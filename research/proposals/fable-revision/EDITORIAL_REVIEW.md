# Independent editorial review of the current slice

This is Codex's review, not Fable-generated writing. Baseline content hash: `510ec90a46d1e15c0d0a77ce5386e3951460bbff70d5d137538e3246b23a1649`. The owner has rejected the earlier restraint in the writing and explicitly requested longer, emotionally alive AI-safety advocacy. A prior twenty-word prompt ceiling is no longer a creative goal.

## Main problem

The existing slice explains its mechanisms more clearly than it makes the player experience them. Many scenes contain a compressed proposition and a neat final aphorism. A clinic exists, a programme accelerates, an IPO funds housing, a grid fails; the people inside these events scarcely speak or act. Longer prose should supply a decision happening under pressure, not simply append another paragraph of explanation.

## Scene-specific direction

- **S1-01/S1-12:** Keep ordinary comic inconvenience. Length alone will not improve the opening; warmth, an incidental human detail and a slightly absurd employer establish what later disappears. Do not announce the genre twist immediately.
- **S2-01/S2-03:** Name the human situation clearly without inventing additional people at risk. The loop needs physical causality the reader can picture, not just the philosophical label “means.” Its current engine stipulates casualties; prose must not claim the missing rail-physics contract has been implemented.
- **S3-01/S3-04:** Let the help happen through recognizable actions and reactions. The ambulance and the person returning to a family give the warning something worth defending. Do not immediately smother each benefit with an essay about how it fails to prove unrestricted safety.
- **S4-03/S4-28:** Give the researcher, auditor and executive different pressures. Mathematical success is not the emotional conflict by itself. The permission must have a recipient, a scope and an enforceable limit, while the threatened delay costs something the player understands. Research permission and successor-deployment permission remain distinct decisions.
- **S5-06:** The current financing premise arrives too abruptly. Explain the specific fictional deal: what the investors require, why contractors depend on it, who loses wages or a home when it collapses. Make those costs forceful, not a parenthetical concession before the obvious lesson. Do not invent measured unemployment numbers or additional deaths.
- **S5-27:** “Both sides cite the other” is too comfortable an aerial view. Put the player in a room with a defence warning they cannot dismiss, evidence they cannot independently inspect, and a concrete reciprocal-inspection option. The warning can be frightening and confidently spoken by a fictional official without becoming an omniscient assertion of real Chinese intentions. The existing inspected option still has its defined effects.
- **S6-10/S6-22:** The strongest horror is an executable attempt to stop followed by an actual override, or an unanswered call to people the player dismissed. These should hurt before a cheerful response arrives. Avoid using every late beat as another office joke. A maintained fallback must still work when its recorded conditions hold.
- **S7-04/S7-19:** The final decisions need exhaustion, the desire to let somebody else take responsibility, and the fear of what happens after the controller leaves. A blanket claim that nobody can ever say no would erase the genuinely different ending paths. Do not make every outcome rhetorically identical to extinction.

## Remove immersion-breaking qualifications, preserve causal information

Receipts currently repeat “fictional,” “authored,” “not a forecast,” and model-calibration explanations. Those are valuable in the source/assumption layer and should be reached deliberately. They should not be the emotional voice of a character, a dilemma, or a consequence. In particular S3-04, S4-03, S4-28, S5-06 and S5-27 carry extensive source-boundary prose that should stay outside the main dramatic exchange.

Not every conditional sentence is a hedge. S6-10's two authority-dependent answers are necessary: the assistant obeys while the human office retains control and refuses after effective control is lost. S7-04 likewise distinguishes a voluntary transfer from ratification. These are causal facts, not neutralizing rhetoric. Preserve them through distinct state-specific lines instead of collapsing all routes into one evasive generic warning.

“I cannot verify either warning” in S5-27 is an important epistemic fact but currently has little dramatic force. It should occur inside a concrete conflict over evidence and defence, rather than serve as an excuse to float above that conflict. Strong advocacy does not require pretending uncertainty is absent; it requires making the demand to race on uncertain assurances disturbing.

## Integration checks for returned drafts

Check all fourteen IDs and their original options/advice slots; prompt ≤1600 characters, advice answer ≤1600, optional authored `reasoning` ≤800, labels ≤12 words. The `reasoning` field is fictional authored assessment, never an actual model's hidden thought process. Preserve provider output separately before editing.

Review invented human details for conflicts with the current branch. No persistent named cast, additional casualties, new military outcomes, universal government control, extra choice, new probability, or newly functional inspection mechanism can be smuggled in as description. If a better scene requires such a mechanic, retain it as a proposed expansion. More feeling should reveal the existing consequences, not counterfeit new engine support.

Finally, vary construction. A sequence of fourteen versions of “a family needs this, the board wants that, what will you sign?” would remain mechanical even at one hundred words each. Change who speaks, the proximity of the harm, the kind of evidence, the source of the pressure and the rhythm of the choice.
