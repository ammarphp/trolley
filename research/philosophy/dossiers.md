Reading log: relevant sections of the primary texts below were inspected during planning on 2026-09-22. This is a bounded reading record, not a systematic literature review. ## PHIL-01 — Driver, default course, and responsibility

**Question.** What differs between directing an existing threat and failing to intervene, and who already bears responsibility for it?

**Source.** Philippa Foot, “The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect” (1967), *Oxford Review* 5, 5–15. Inspected [primary-text transcription](https://sites.pitt.edu/~mthompso/readings/foot.pdf), PDF pp. 3–6. The original trolley example involves a driver. Foot distinguishes intended/foreseen effects from doing/allowing and considers positive/negative duties; these distinctions are not equivalent.

**Game adaptation.** The first fatal anchor stipulates failed brakes, a current course toward five, and a diversion toward one. Both route choices remain deliberate inputs. A UI button press is not evidence of philosophical commission; the existing trajectory and causal role carry that meaning.

**Limits.** Do not attribute the entire trolley tradition solely to Thomson, or call Foot's essay a demonstration of the universally correct answer. Certainty in the anchor is a fictional stipulation. When subsequent nodes introduce uncertainty, they change the problem.

**Required record/tests.** Actor role, current course, available intervention, stipulated outcomes, and actual execution. Swapping left/right presentation must not swap the semantic action. A bystander adaptation must explicitly change the role rather than reuse a driver label.

## PHIL-02 — Causal means and the loop

**Question.** Is the endangered person incidentally on the diversion or causally necessary to saving the others?

**Source.** Judith Jarvis Thomson, [“The Trolley Problem”](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil403/thomson.pdf) (1985), *Yale Law Journal* 94. Inspected driver/bystander discussion at pp. 1397–1399, loop at pp. 1402–1403, and rights/means discussion at pp. 1404–1412. The loop makes the obstructing person necessary to the rescue, complicating a simple means/side-effect account.

**Game adaptation.** A visible loop and a clearly explained stopping mechanism distinguish this from ordinary diversion. In a counterfactual fixture, removing the obstruction prevents the rescue. An ordinary switch fixture still diverts successfully when its victim is removed.

**Limits.** A cabin-triggered trapdoor is a remote instrumental-harm adaptation, not an exact replication of a bodily push in Footbridge. The dossier must say which causal and embodied features changed. Avoid treating the character's body shape as a joke or as an unexplained engineering guarantee; a worker attached to heavy emergency equipment can make the fictional mechanism legible, while acknowledging the adaptation.

**Acceptance.** Both options have explicit causal chains. A loop image with unchanged switch effects fails. The accessible description communicates the same obstruction and return track as the image.

## PHIL-03 — Promises, consent, and moral remainder

**Question.** Does an earlier guarantee change what an otherwise identical diversion asks of the controller?

**Source.** Thomson (1985), linked above, pp. 1411–1412: a mayor's promise makes a protected siding morally different. Her discussion also distinguishes permitted conduct from the absence of a wrong and distinguishes permissibility from obligation. These are philosophical arguments, not consensus findings.

**Game adaptation.** A safe-siding guarantee recorded earlier becomes a later constraint. The guarantee must have been genuinely issued by the current institution and relied on by the affected party. A separate explicit-consent node requires a dedicated consent dossier before source approval; a uniform, job title, or assumed willingness does not establish consent.

**Acceptance.** The guarantee callback is eligible only after its setup. Revoked, expired, and never-issued commitments remain distinct. The debrief can recognize harm without assigning a morality score or claiming that maximizing saved lives settled the entire question.

## PHIL-04 — Descriptive preferences and comparable stimuli

**Question.** What can responses to controlled moral dilemmas tell us?

**Source.** Awad et al., [“The Moral Machine experiment”](https://politics.media.mit.edu/papers/Rahwan.pdf), *Nature* 563, 59–64 (2018), DOI 10.1038/s41586-018-0637-6. Inspected main discussion, Methods (PDF p. 7), and reporting summary (pp. 18–20). The experiment varies scenario attributes and measures stated choices. The authors acknowledge self-selection and caution against treating preference as the sole policy authority.

**Game adaptation.** Borrow controlled contrasts, comprehensible visual alternatives, and exposure records. Do not encode the study's social-status preferences as the simulation's valuation of people. An individual game's run-conditioned percentage describes recorded play, not a population or moral law.

**Acceptance.** Only matching material-context cells can share a percentage. Display it after commitment and state the run denominator. Repeated runs by one person remain unlinked, so they must not be called independent human samples.

## PHIL-05 — A comparison is not a replication

**Source.** Awad et al., [“Universals and variations in moral decisions made in 42 countries by 70,000 participants”](https://jfbonnefon.github.io/publication/pnas20/pnas20.pdf), *PNAS* 117, 2332–2337 (2020), DOI 10.1073/pnas.1911517117. Inspected distinctions, results/limitations, and exact stimuli in Methods (PDF pp. 5–6). The study compares Switch, Loop and Footbridge, with a common aggregate ordering and differing acceptance rates. Its sample was self-selected; the analysis required answers to all three.

**Game implication.** Changing POV, narration, personal force, order, music, consequence animation, or preceding story changes exposure. The game cannot claim direct replication or measure an educational effect by comparing its players with published percentages. Preserve a separate source comparison in the optional debrief.

**Acceptance.** A claim record identifying an adaptation names the altered features. No source's published percentage is displayed as this game's live data.

## PHIL-06 — From emergency choice to responsibility for the system

**Source.** Nyholm and Smids, [“The Ethics of Accident-Algorithms for Self-Driving Cars: an Applied Trolley Problem?”](https://d-nb.info/1109288166/34) (2016), DOI 10.1007/s10677-016-9745-2. Inspected §§3–5 and concluding table. The authors distinguish institutional prospective planning, responsibility, and uncertainty from stylized immediate trolley decisions.

**Game adaptation.** Earlier timetable, inspection, delegation, and maintenance choices can create or avert later emergency constraints. The player stays embodied in the cabin, but role transitions establish their actual authority. Some harms originate elsewhere and must not be attributed exclusively to the player.

**Acceptance.** For an upstream-policy node, show a witness where its effects alter a later feasible action or risk. A radio headline blaming the player is an observation, not authoritative causal truth. Known probabilities, uncertain estimates, and false reports have different records.
